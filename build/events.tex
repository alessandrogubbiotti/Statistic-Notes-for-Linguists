\section{Events and Sample Space}
Motivated by the Introduction~\ref{s:intro} we first introduce the notion of events. We then see that events can be modelled using set theory, after introducing the sample space

\subsection{Definition of an event}
In light of the subjective perspective on Probability Theory we will refer to the person who is evaluating the probabilities of events using "you". Following \cite{definetti}  we can define events as 
\begin{definition}
    \label{d:event}
    An event is an unambiguous statement which can be either true or false and about which you are uncertain.
\end{definition}
Informally, unambiguous statement means that a possible bet or insurance based upon it can be decided without question. 
Examples of events are: " The next ChatGPT sentence will be incorrect" or " Napoleon was born on March" ( to decide this last statement, I should open Wikipedia). 
Events will be usually denoted by capital letters, $A$, $B, \ldots$, and the impossible event will be usually denoted by $\emptyset$. 




\subsection{Operations and relations between events}
\label{ss:operations}
From two events $E$ and $F$ it is possible to build new events using the operations "and", "or" and "not", namely, to consider "$E$ and $F$", "$E$ or $F$" and "not $E$". We will write them as 
\begin{equation}
	\label{e:and_or_not}
	E \wedge F = \text{"$E$ and $F$"}, \qquad E \vee F = \text{"$E$ or $F$"}, \qquad \neg E = \text{"not $E$"}.
\end{equation}
The event $E \wedge F$ is true when both $E$ and $F$ are true, $E \vee F$ is true when at least one of the two is true (possibly both), and $\neg E$ is true when $E$ is false. To make a simple example, let $E$ = "Tomorrow it will rain" and $F$ = "The coin I am about to toss will show heads", the two events of the Introduction. Then 
\begin{itemize}
	\item $E \wedge F$ = "Tomorrow it will rain and the coin will show heads".
	\item $E \vee F$ = "Either tomorrow it will rain or the coin will show heads (or both)".
	\item $\neg E$ = "Tomorrow it will not rain", $\neg F$ = "The coin will not show heads".
\end{itemize}

Given two events $E$ and $F$ we say that $E$ \emph{implies} $F$, and we write $E \rightarrow F$, if $F$ is true whenever $E$ is. If $G$ = "Tomorrow it will rain heavily", then $G \rightarrow E$. Note that in general 
\begin{itemize}
	\item $E \wedge F \rightarrow E$ and $E \wedge F \rightarrow F$;
	\item $E \rightarrow E \vee F$ and $F \rightarrow E \vee F$;
	\item if $E$ is impossible, then $E \rightarrow F$ for every event $F$, and if $F$ is certain, then $E \rightarrow F$ for every event $E$.
\end{itemize}
The first two relations say that "and" makes events smaller (harder to be true), while "or" makes them bigger. 

\sidenote{Different sentences can define the same event: $E =$ "The die shows an even number" and $F =$ "The die shows 2, 4 or 6" are the same event. We identify those two propositions with the same event  and we write $E = F$. In particular, any two impossible events are equal and we denote them $\emptyset$.}



\subsection{Algebra of events}
\label{ss:algebra}

There are no inherent restrictions on the events one can define and on which you could try to assign a probability. When doing a probabilistic study, however, you fix beforehand the events you want to consider, and this choice depends on what you are looking at. If we are interested in the result of a coin toss, we want to consider the event "The coin shows heads", but not the event "The coin shows heads and tomorrow it will rain in Rome". 

\begin{example}[The shop]
	\label{ex:shop}
	You are paid by a shop owner to perform a statistical analysis of the customers visiting his shop. For time and economic constraints, you have to choose what kind of events you are interested in. One possibility is to consider only the number of customers, so that the events will be of the form "More than 10 customers will visit the shop", "3 customers will visit the shop", "Between 5 and 7 customers will visit the shop", ... 
	If the shop owner is also interested in the gender of the customers, you can do a finer analysis and consider events that take into account both the number of customers and their gender, such as "More than 3 male customers and less than 10 female customers will visit the shop" or "2 male and 1 female customers will visit the shop". 
%Note that every event of the first kind is also an event of the second kind: "3 customers will visit the shop" is the same event as "3 customers will visit the shop, of which either 0 male and 3 female, or 1 male and 2 female, or 2 male and 1 female, or 3 male and 0 female". The second choice is therefore finer than the first one, in the sense that it contains more events. We will come back to this in Section~\ref{ss:refinement}. 
\end{example}

The events we want to consider are called \emph{measurable events}, since they are the ones that our observation is able to decide (in Example~\ref{ex:shop}, by counting the customers) and, as we will see, the ones to which we assign a probability. Their set will be denoted by $\mathfrak F$. 

The choice of $\mathfrak F$ is free, up to one requirement: if we want to consider $E$ and $F$, we also have to consider $E \wedge F$, $E \vee F$ and $\neg E$. One of the reasons for this is that whatever we are looking at in order to decide whether $E$ and $F$ are true decides also whether $E \wedge F$, $E \vee F$ and $\neg E$ are true. In Example~\ref{ex:shop}, if by counting the customers we can decide $E$ = "More than 10 customers will visit the shop" and $F$ = "Less than 20 customers will visit the shop", by counting the customers we can also decide $E \wedge F$ = "Between 11 and 19 customers will visit the shop" and $\neg E$ = "At most 10 customers will visit the shop". 

We observe that when we will be introducing a probability in Section~\ref{s:probability}, we need to introduce it in such a way that the probabilities of $E \wedge F$, $E \vee F$ and $\neg E$ are tied to the ones of $E$ and $F$. For instance, the probability of $\neg E$ is one minus the probability of $E$: assigning a probability to $E$ means assigning a probability to $\neg E$ as well, whether we want it or not. Finally, we ask that the certain event belongs to $\mathfrak F$: it is decided by any observation whatsoever, and it is the event to which we will give probability $1$ and with which all the other probabilities are compared. 

\begin{definition}[Measurable events]
	\label{d:measurable}
	The events we want to consider are called \emph{measurable events}. Their set $\mathfrak F$ is an \emph{algebra of events}, that is, 
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item the certain event $\Omega$ belongs to $\mathfrak F$;
		\item if $E \in \mathfrak F$, then $\neg E \in \mathfrak F$;
		\item if $E, F \in \mathfrak F$, then $E \wedge F \in \mathfrak F$ and $E \vee F \in \mathfrak F$.\sidenote{If we also ask that $E_1 \vee E_2 \vee E_3 \vee \ldots$ belongs to $\mathfrak F$ whenever $E_1, E_2, E_3, \ldots$ do, $\mathfrak F$ is called a $\sigma$-algebra of events. This is the notion used in measure theory.}
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The algebra of a single event]
	\label{ex:cointoss}
	Suppose that we want to consider only one event $E$. By Definition~\ref{d:measurable}, $\mathfrak F$ has to contain also $\neg E$, $\Omega$ and $\emptyset$, and 
	\begin{equation}
		\label{e:cointoss}
		\mathfrak F = \{ \emptyset, E, \neg E, \Omega \}
	\end{equation}
	is an algebra of events: $E \wedge \neg E = \emptyset$, $E \vee \neg E = \Omega$, $\neg(\neg E) = E$, and "and", "or" and "not" of events of $\mathfrak F$ give again events of $\mathfrak F$, as you can check with the rules \eqref{e:rules}. It is the smallest algebra containing $E$. 
	For instance, if we toss a coin and we are interested only in the face it shows, then $E$ = "The coin shows heads", $\neg E$ = "The coin shows tails", $\Omega$ = "The coin shows either heads or tails", and \eqref{e:cointoss} is the algebra of the coin toss. 
\end{example}


\begin{example}[The shop, continued]
	\label{ex:shop_algebra}
	In the first choice of Example~\ref{ex:shop}, the measurable events are all the statements about the number of customers, such as "More than 10 customers will visit the shop", "Between 5 and 7 customers will visit the shop" or "An even number of customers will visit the shop", and their set is an algebra of events: "and", "or" and "not" of statements about the number of customers are again statements about the number of customers. We denote this algebra by $\mathfrak F_1$. In the second choice, the measurable events are all the statements about the number of male customers and the number of female customers, and their set $\mathfrak F_2$ is again an algebra of events. 
%Since a statement about the number of customers is also a statement about the numbers of male and female customers, every event of $\mathfrak F_1$ is an event of $\mathfrak F_2$. 
\end{example}





\subsection{Elementary events and sample space}
\label{ss:elementary}

Once the events we want to consider are fixed and collected in $\mathfrak F$, it is possible to define a particular kind of events, the \emph{elementary events}, thanks to which we can model events using set theory. Informally, elementary events are the smallest among the events we want to consider, and can be regarded as the outcomes of the "experiment" we are observing. It is indeed possible to define a notion of smallness using the relation "implies" introduced in Section~\ref{ss:operations}: an event $E$ is smaller than $F$ if $E \rightarrow F$, since $E$ is true in fewer circumstances than $F$. The impossible event is the smallest of all, since $\emptyset \rightarrow F$ for every $F$, and the elementary events are the smallest ones after it. 
\begin{definition}[Elementary events]
	\label{d:elementary}
	An event $E \in \mathfrak F$ is \emph{elementary} if it is not impossible and the only events of $\mathfrak F$ that imply it are $\emptyset$ and $E$ itself: if $F \in \mathfrak F$ and $F \rightarrow E$, then $F = \emptyset$ or $F = E$. 
\end{definition}
For instance, if $\mathfrak F$ consists of all the statements about the result of a die, "The result is 6" is elementary, while "The result is an even number" is not, since "The result is 2" implies it. 
\begin{ExerciseList}
	\Exercise Show that two different elementary events are mutually exclusive, that is, that if $E$ and $F$ are elementary and $E \neq F$, then $E \wedge F = \emptyset$. 
	\Answer By Definition~\ref{d:measurable}, $E \wedge F \in \mathfrak F$. Since $E \wedge F \rightarrow E$ and $E$ is elementary, either $E \wedge F = \emptyset$ or $E \wedge F = E$. In the second case $E \rightarrow F$ and, since $F$ is elementary and $E \neq \emptyset$, $E = F$, which is excluded. Hence $E \wedge F = \emptyset$. 
	\Exercise In the algebra $\mathfrak F_1$ of Example~\ref{ex:shop_algebra}, which of the following events are elementary? "No customer will visit the shop", "At most one customer will visit the shop", "Exactly 3 customers will visit the shop". 
\end{ExerciseList}

The notion of elementary event is fundamental since it allows to encode the events of $\mathfrak F$ as sets, see Section~\ref{ss:subset}. 
\begin{definition}[Sample space]
	\label{d:sample_space}
	Given $\mathfrak F$, the \emph{sample space} is the set of the elementary events of $\mathfrak F$. We denote it by $\Omega$, the same symbol used for the certain event.\sidenote{The two uses of $\Omega$ are reconciled in Section~\ref{ss:subset}, where the certain event is identified with the set of all the elementary events.} 
\end{definition}
Elements of the sample space, that is, elementary events, are usually denoted by $\omega$. The sample space can be regarded as the set of the possible outcomes of the system you are looking at, with the degree of precision that you want to impose. From now on we assume that every event of $\mathfrak F$ which is not impossible is implied by at least one elementary event.\sidenote{This is always the case when $\mathfrak F$ is finite, and it is the case in all the examples of these notes. There are algebras of events with no elementary event at all: the statements about an infinite sequence of coin tosses that involve only finitely many tosses, see Example~\ref{ex:infinite_coin}, form one, since a condition on one more toss makes any such statement strictly smaller.} Some examples are in order. 

\begin{example}[Sample space of a coin toss]
	\label{ex:coin_sample}
	If $\mathfrak F$ is the algebra of the coin toss \eqref{e:cointoss}, the elementary events are $\omega_1 = E$ = "The coin shows heads" and $\omega_2 = \neg E$ = "The coin shows tails", and the sample space is $\Omega = \{\omega_1, \omega_2\}$. Note that we can use a different notation and set $1 = \omega_1$ and $0 = \omega_2$. However, $0$ and $1$ must not be mistaken for numbers: they are symbols indicating elementary events.\sidenote{Despite that almost every textbook in probability starts by providing the sample space, the sample space in probability is only used as a mean of ground events to a standard object, see Subsection~\ref{ss:subet}} 
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A die is rolled]
	\label{ex:die_sample}
	If a die is rolled and $\mathfrak F$ consists of all the statements about the result, such as "The result is an even number", the elementary events are the ones that cannot be decomposed just by looking at the die, $1$ = "The result is 1", \ldots, $6$ = "The result is 6". The sample space is therefore $\Omega = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ and, again, the elements $1, \ldots, 6$ must not be mistaken for numbers, since they are elementary events and, for instance, $1 + 3$ doesn't make sense. 
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The shop, continued]
	\label{ex:shop_sample}
	In the algebra $\mathfrak F_1$ of Example~\ref{ex:shop_algebra} the elementary events are $E_n$ = "Exactly $n$ customers will visit the shop", for $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$: a statement about the number of customers that implies $E_n$ is either $E_n$ itself or impossible. The sample space $\Omega = \{E_0, E_1, E_2, \ldots\}$, which we can also write as $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$, has infinitely many elements. In the algebra $\mathfrak F_2$ the elementary events are $E_{m,f}$ = "Exactly $m$ male and $f$ female customers will visit the shop", and the sample space is the set of the pairs $(m,f)$. 
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A chess match]
	If you observe a chess match, the events you want to consider are those that involve the game alone, such as "The game will end before 10 moves are taken" or "Black wins", while the elementary events are the possible single games. Therefore, the sample space is the set of all possible games. A full description of a game is usually given in terms of the so called algebraic notation; for instance, the fifth game of the 1972 World Championship between Spassky and Fischer is \\

\subsection{Events as subsets of the sample space}
\label{ss:subset}

\begin{definition}[Elementary events in favour of an event]
	\label{d:favour}
	We say that an elementary event $\omega \in \Omega$ is \emph{in favour} of $E \in \mathfrak F$ if $\omega \rightarrow E$ ($\omega$ implies $E$). 
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
The minimality of the elementary events implies that for each $E \in \mathfrak F$ and $\omega \in \Omega$ either $\omega$ is in favour of $E$ or the two are mutually exclusive, $\omega \wedge E = \emptyset$: indeed $\omega \wedge E \rightarrow \omega$, so that $\omega \wedge E = \emptyset$ or $\omega \wedge E = \omega$, and in the second case $\omega \rightarrow E$.
\end{remark}

We can associate to each event the subset of $\Omega$ whose elements are the elementary events in its favour: 
\begin{equation}
	\label{e:abstract_to_concrete}
	\begin{array}{ccc}
		\Phi: \mathfrak F & \to & \{ A \, , A \subset \Omega\} \\
		E & \mapsto & \{ \omega \in \Omega \, , \omega \textrm{is in favour of} E \}. 
	\end{array}
\end{equation}

\begin{example}[Die]
	\label{ex:die_subset}
	We have seen in Example~\ref{ex:die_sample} that the sample space of a rolled die can be identified with $\Omega = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$. The elementary events in favour of $E$ = "The result is an odd number" are $1, 3, 5$. Therefore we identify $E$ with the subset $\Phi(E) = \{1,3,5\}$ of $\Omega$. 
\end{example}

The fundamental fact about $\Phi$ is that the operations "and", "or" and "not" correspond to the operations $\cap$, $\cup$ and $()^c$ between subsets of $\Omega$, that "implies" corresponds to "is a subset of", and that different events correspond to different subsets. 
\begin{proposition}
	\label{p:subset}
	Let $E, F \in \mathfrak F$. Then 
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\Phi(E \wedge F) = \Phi(E) \cap \Phi(F)$, $\Phi(E \vee F) = \Phi(E) \cup \Phi(F)$ and $\Phi(\neg E) = \Phi(E)^c$, the set of the elementary events that are not in $\Phi(E)$;
		\item $E \rightarrow F$ if and only if $\Phi(E) \subset \Phi(F)$; 
		\item $E = F$ if and only if $\Phi(E) = \Phi(F)$. 
	\end{enumerate}
	Moreover, $\Phi(\emptyset)$ is the empty set and $\Phi(\Omega) = \Omega$, the whole sample space. 
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	Since $E \wedge F$ is true exactly when $E$ and $F$ are both true, $\omega \rightarrow E \wedge F$ if and only if $\omega \rightarrow E$ and $\omega \rightarrow F$, which is the first identity. For the third one, if $\omega \rightarrow E$ and $\omega \rightarrow \neg E$, then $\omega \rightarrow E \wedge \neg E = \emptyset$, and $\omega$ would be impossible: hence $\omega \in \Phi(\neg E)$ implies $\omega \notin \Phi(E)$. Conversely, if $\omega \notin \Phi(E)$, then $\omega \wedge E = \emptyset$ and, by the rules \eqref{e:rules}, $\omega = \omega \wedge (E \vee \neg E) = (\omega \wedge E) \vee (\omega \wedge \neg E) = \omega \wedge \neg E$, that is, $\omega \rightarrow \neg E$. The second identity follows from the other two, since $E \vee F = \neg(\neg E \wedge \neg F)$ by de Morgan's laws. 
	As for point 3, suppose that $\Phi(E) = \Phi(F)$ but $E \neq F$. Then one between $E \wedge \neg F$ and $F \wedge \neg E$ is not impossible, since $E \wedge \neg F = \emptyset$ gives $E = (E \wedge F) \vee (E \wedge \neg F) = E \wedge F$, that is, $E \rightarrow F$, and in the same way $F \wedge \neg E = \emptyset$ gives $F \rightarrow E$. Say that $E \wedge \neg F \neq \emptyset$. By the assumption made after Definition~\ref{d:sample_space}, there is an elementary event $\omega \rightarrow E \wedge \neg F$, so that $\omega \in \Phi(E)$ and $\omega \notin \Phi(F)$, which contradicts $\Phi(E) = \Phi(F)$. 
	Point 2 follows: $E \rightarrow F$ if and only if $E \wedge F = E$ (see the exercises of Section~\ref{ss:algebra}), if and only if $\Phi(E) \cap \Phi(F) = \Phi(E)$ by points 1 and 3, if and only if $\Phi(E) \subset \Phi(F)$. Finally, no elementary event is in favour of $\emptyset$, since elementary events are not impossible, and every elementary event is in favour of $\Omega$. 
\end{proof}

From now on we will consider $\mathfrak F$ as a set of subsets of $\Omega$, that is, we will not distinguish between an event $E$ and the set $\Phi(E)$ of the elementary events in its favour. This allows us to depict events, their unions and their intersections with Venn's diagrams, as~Figure~\ref{f:Venn} shows.

\subsection{Relevant Examples}

In this examples we provide the basic examples of sample spaces to which we will come back throughout the notes: the \emph{Bernoulli trial} space and the sample space of a random number. 

\begin{model}[A Coin Toss]
If in a coin toss we are interested only in the face the coin shows, as we always are when we speak about coin tosses, then the only two non-tricial events we want to consider are  0=" The result is tails" and 1 = " The result is heads", see also Example~\ref{ex:cointoss} and \ref{ex:die_subset}. Therefore 
	\begin{equation}
		\Omega_1=\{0,1\},
	\end{equation}
	and we will ofter refer to $1$ as to a success. The set of events that we want to consider can be represented by 
	\begin{equation}
		\mathfrak F = \{\emptyset, \{0\}, \{1\},\{0,1\}\}.
	\end{equation}
\end{model}

\begin{model}[two, three and $n$ coin tosses]
	\label{ex:n_coin}
The sample space of two coin tosses can be encoded by $\Omega_2=\{00,01,10,11\}= \{x_1x_2\,,\, x_i\in\{0,1\} i=1,2\}$, where, for example, 
If we toss twice a coin and we are only interested in the faces they show, then we are interested in events of the form " The first coin shows heads" or "The second coin shows tails". The smallest events we can make by intersecting this kind of events are of the form 01="The first toss gave tails and the second tails". Therefore the sample space is constituted by the set 
	\bel{}{
		\Omega_2 = \{00,01,10,11\} = \{x_1x_2, x_i \in \{0,1\}, \text{ for $i = 1, 2$}  \} 
	} 
	of two digit string where every digit represents the outcome of the respective coin. 
	Similarly, for 3 coin tosses 
	\bel{}{\Omega_3=\{000,001,010,011,100,101,110,111\}=\{x_1x_2x_3\,,\,x_i\in\{0,1\} i=1,2,3\},} 
	 and, in the general case of $n$ coin tosses, the sample space is the space of strings of $n$ digit with values in $\{0,1\}$, namely 
	\begin{equation}
		\label{e:ntoss}
		\Omega_n = \{x_1x_2\ldots x_n\, , x_i \in \{0,1\} , i = 1,\ldots, n\}. 
	\end{equation}
	Events that we will often use when introducing the Binomial distribution, see Example~\ref{????} are the events $E_k$= "There has been exactly $k$ successes", for $k \in \mathbb N$, $k < n$. For instance, if $n = 3$ and $k = 1$, is $E_1=\{001,010,100\}=\{x_1x_2x_3\,,\,x_1+x_2+x_3=1\}$. The particular encoding given by \eqref{e:ntoss} allows us to write in simple terms the condition imposed by $E_k$:
	\begin{equation}
	\label{e:ntoss_ksuccesses}
		E_k = \{ x_1\ldots x_n \in \Omega_n \,, x_1 + \ldots + x_n = k \}.
	\end{equation}
\end{model}

\begin{model}[Finite but Undetermined Amount of Coin Tosses]
If we are observing a finite amount of coin tosses, but we do not know how many of them there will be, we can define the set of possible outcomes, that is, the sample space, via the following encoding  
\begin{equation}
    \label{e:finite_coin}
	\begin{split}
	\Omega= & \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty \Omega_i = \{0,1,00,01,10,11,000, \ldots \},\\
		& = \left\{ x_1\ldots x_n , n \in \mathbb N, x_i \in \{0,1\}, \text{ for $i = 1,\ldots n$}\right\}
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
	where $\Omega_i$ has been defined \ref{e:ntoss}. 

\end{model}
\begin{remark}
	\label{r:uncountable}
The above examples had the important property that their elements could be written as a list $\Omega = \{\omega_1, \ldots, \omega_n\}$ for some $n \in \mathbb N$, in the finite case, or $\Omega = \{\omega_1, \ldots, \omega_n, \ldots\}$ in the infinite case. In other words, we have decided a rule to count its elements, and, we have managed to come across all the elements of $\Omega$ with this rule. This is no longer possible in the next examples (try yourself to enumerate the elements of $\Omega$). In mathematical terms, in the next two examples $\Omega$ is uncountably infinite.
\end{remark}

\begin{model}[Infinite Coin Tosses]
	\label{ex:infinite_coin}
If we are observing an infinite amount of coin tosses, then the sample space is the set of strings whose digits denote the result of the corresponding coin. 
	\begin{equation}
		\label{e:infinite_coin}
 \Omega=\{x_1...x_n...\, ,\, x_i\in\{0,1\}\}.
	\end{equation}
	 Possible events that we would like to take into account are of the form "The first toss gave $a_1$, the second $a_2$,..., the nth $a_n$", where $n \in \mathbb N$ and $a_{i} \in \{0,1\}$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$. This event correspods to the subset of $\Omega$ consisting on strings whose first $n$ elements are $a_i$, for $i = 1,\ldots, n$
		\begin{equation}
			\label{e:infinite_coin_set}
			A_{a_1,\ldots, a_n} = \{x_1\ldots x_n\ldots \in \Omega\,\, x_1 = a_1, \ldots, x_n = a_n \}.
		\end{equation}
\end{model}

\begin{model}[ Random Number Between 0 and 1]
	\label{ex:unif}
	By typing on R the command runif, the computer gives us a number $X$ between 0 and 1. 
\begin{knitrout}
\definecolor{shadecolor}{rgb}{0.969, 0.969, 0.969}\color{fgcolor}\begin{kframe}
\begin{alltt}
\hlkwd{runif}\hldef{(}\hlnum{1}\hldef{)}
\end{alltt}
\begin{verbatim}
## [1] 0.8192029
\end{verbatim}
\end{kframe}
\end{knitrout}
	Some events in which we are usually interested are " $X < 1/2$ ", " $X \in [1/4, 1/ 2) $". By intesecating the above events we can see, at least intuitively, that the smallest events one can make are $X = x$. The sample space is therefore composed by the events "$ X = a $" for each $a \in [0,1]$, and, by denoting $a$ the event $ X = a$, 
	\bel{e:unif_1}{
		\Omega = \{\text{" $X = a$"}, a \in [0,1]\} = [0,1].
	} 
The set of events we want to consider $\mathfrak F$ contains the events of the form $ X \in [a,b]$, where $0 \leq a <  b \leq 1 $, if we are using the notation in the centre of \eqref{e:unif_1}, and $[a,b]$ if we are using the other notation for $\Omega$.    
\end{model}

	Example~\ref{ex:infinite_coin} and ~\ref{ex:unif} constitute are different from the previous ones. In both cases the spaces are too big to be written as a list of elements, that is, in the form $\Omega = \{ \omega_1 , \omega_2, \ldots \}$. In jargon, $\Omega$ is not discrete. The difficulty is that now the specification of $\mathfrak F$ becomes important since, for good  mathematical reasons, it is in general not possible to consider each $A \subset \Omega$.

	\label{exercise:game_2}
    (\cite{Ross} Chapter 2 Exercise 2) In an experiment, die is rolled continually until a 6 appears, at which point the experiment stops. What is the sample space of this experiment? Let $E_n$ denote the event " The experiment is completed before n rolls". Write it as a subset of the sample space. Describe $\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty E_i\right)^c$.

\Answer The sample space is the space of outputs of the experiment. We encode the event "The first roll gave 4, the second 2, the third 6 and then stops" in the string 426. That is, we encode the output of the experiment in strings of digits 1,2...,5,6,  where the digit correspond to the result of the die, and the position of the digit corresponds to when has the digit appeared. The string must stop at after the first 6, since the game stops when the die gives 6. \\We have to keep in mind that we can observe events in which we never stop rolling the die, which correspond to infinite strings of 1,2...5, for example 1212121.... or  121233...34453236.... 
\bel{}{\Omega=\{ 6, 16, 26, 36, 46, 56,116,...\}\cup\{x_1x_2....x_n...\,: x_{i}\in\{1,2,3,4,5\} \}=E\cup G.}
The set $E$ is the event that the game stops in a finite number of rolls. The set $G$ is the event that the game goes on without ending. For any reasonable probability, the event G has probability 0, and it is usually neglected. 
The events $E_i$ can be written as subsets of the state space in the following way: $E_1=\{6\}$, $E_2=\{6,16,26...,56\}$, $E_3=E_2 \cup \{116,126,...,556\}$,... (In a more compact form $E_i=\{x_1...x_n6\,:\, x_i\in\{1,...,5\}\, n\leq i\}$).

The event $$\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty E_n= E_0\cup E_1\cup E_2,....= "E_0\,\, or E_1 \,\,or ....E_n \,\,\,or ..."$$
is, by definition of "or", the event that $E_n$ happens for at least one $n$. That is, is the event "The game ends before $n$ rolls at least one $n$ "="The game ends after $n$ rolls for some $n$", which coincides with $E$.
The set event $E^c$ corresponds to $G$.
